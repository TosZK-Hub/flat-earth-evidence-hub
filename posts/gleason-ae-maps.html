Azimuthal Equidistant & Gleason-Style Maps

North-polar azimuthal equidistant (AE) maps — including Alexander Gleason’s 1892 New Standard Map of the World and the United Nations emblem — are among the most recognisable FE visuals. This page steelmans why the community treats them as “the real map,” then states what the projection and Gleason’s own patent actually are.

In this lesson we treat azimuthal equidistant maths as established projection geometry, and Gleason-style disc-Earth readings as hypothesis — with southern-route issues labelled carefully.

Side-by-side claim card

FE steelman

A north-centred circular map with Antarctica as an ice ring matches lived northern navigation, compass behaviour toward a single pole, and the UN’s own emblem. Gleason marketed a “scientifically and practically correct” flat circular chart. Southern flight “detours” look shorter on AE posters than polar routes — so the disc is treated as the physical layout, not a drawing trick.

Hypothesis when AE is read as a planet survey.

Globe-model account

Azimuthal equidistant from the North Pole is a standard projection of a sphere: distances and bearings from the centre are true; shapes and southern distances stretch without limit toward the rim. The UN emblem is officially described as that projection. Gleason’s US patent 497,917 claims a time-dial and pivoted arms on a circular map — not Earth’s shape — and his specification says the map is an “extorsion” (distortion) from a globe.

Established for projection math + patent text + UN description.

What would settle it

  • Timed southern great-circle flights (e.g. Sydney–Santiago) vs AE-rim path lengths.
  • Geodesic distance tables for southern city pairs vs AE-measured rim arcs.
  • Read Gleason patent claim section: which sentence, if any, asserts a flat planet?

The FE case (steelmanned)

Modern FE organisers place a Gleason-style disc on posters and stages because it makes Antarctica a perimeter “ice wall,” puts the North Pole at the centre, and visually matches the UN logo many already trust. Eric Dubay–style proof lists and society wikis catalogue monopole maps (Rowbotham, Gleason, Voliva, etc.) as the geography of a plane world. The steelman is not “any circle is proof”; it is that if Earth were a plane with a north centre, an AE chart is the natural atlas — and mainstream institutions already print that atlas shape.

Well-argued hypothesis

AE is a coherent model map for monopole FE geography. Whether it matches measured southern distances is a separate, testable claim.

What an azimuthal equidistant map is

In cartography, an azimuthal equidistant projection maps the sphere so that all points at geographic distance d from a chosen centre lie on a circle of radius proportional to d, and azimuths from the centre are preserved. Choose the North Pole as centre and you get the familiar flat-Earth poster. Choose another centre (a city, a quake epicentre) and radio operators or seismologists use the same math for different jobs.

Important: producing an AE chart does not require believing the Earth is flat. GIS software emits AE tiles from spherical coordinates every day.

Established evidence

AE is a named, textbook map projection of a sphere. Distortion grows with distance from the centre; Antarctica becomes an enormous ring because southern parallels are stretched.

Gleason’s map and patent — fair read

Alexander Gleason (Buffalo) published circular world maps and, in 1893, received US Patent 497,917 for a “Time-Chart”: a circular dial, longitude graduations, and two pivoted indicator arms. Secondary readings of the patent (e.g. Empirical Earth’s document walkthrough) emphasise:

  • The legal claim covers the mechanical dial/arms — not cosmology.
  • Gleason’s specification describes the sheet as agreeing with “first-class geographical globe maps” and speaks of the “extorsion of the map from that of a globe” by straightening meridians.
  • The map title credits the projection of J. S. Christopher; Gleason’s commercial product sits in ordinary map/time-chart patent classes.

Gleason also wrote flat-Earth advocacy books. Steelman both facts: a flat-Earth author can still file a patent that describes a globe projection mechanically. The patent text is primary; memes that “the Patent Office certified a flat Earth” are not.

Hypothesis

Community use of Gleason’s sheet as a physical planet map.

Speculation

Stories that the patent “proves” NASA later hid a disc world.

The UN emblem

The United Nations describes its emblem as “a map of the world representing an azimuthal equidistant projection centred on the North Pole,” with olive branches, extending to 60° south latitude and including five concentric circles (UN emblem/flag pages; design history via Oliver Lincoln Lundquist’s 1945 team). That is an institutional choice of a projection for a political logo — not a geodetic survey concluding the planet is flat.

Established

UN’s own prose calls it an azimuthal equidistant projection centred on the North Pole.

Clean falsifiers (southern distances)

On a north-polar AE disc used as a physical map, routes between southern cities run along a stretched rim and become longer than routes that dip north. On a globe, many of those pairs have short great-circle paths over the Southern Ocean. Scheduled flights such as Qantas Sydney–Santiago (commonly cited in secondary analyses) complete in times matching roughly 7,000-mile-class great circles — not AE-rim marathon paths. Tabulate block times yourself from airline public schedules; do not take meme graphics as primary.

Gleason himself reportedly sought captains’ data on southern routes — the right kind of question even if modern FE posters skip the timetable test.
